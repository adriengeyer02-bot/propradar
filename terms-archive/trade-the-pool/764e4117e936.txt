 Profit split numbers get top billing on almost every prop firm homepage. A profit split is simply how a funded trader and the firm divide trading profit. It sounds like the whole story. However, it covers only the first line of what a trader actually collects.
Picture two offers side by side, one at 90% and the other at 70%. The choice looks obvious. For example, the 90% firm might pay monthly while the 70% firm pays every two weeks. As a result, the smaller number can put money in a trader’s account sooner.

What Does A Profit Split Actually Mean?
Furthermore, most readers land here after typing one blunt question into a search bar. What does profit split mean? For a funded stock trader, it means keeping an agreed percentage of eligible profit. The firm keeps the rest.
That part takes no finance degree, only a little arithmetic. This 70/30 ratio would send $70 from each $100 in eligible profits to the trader. The evaluation fee, on the other hand, is not part of any of these calculations.
It has been paid by traders at the beginning and does not affect the split. The beginner trader would treat these three factors as a single number. Contrary to many guides available online, this guide considers the split as just another item on the long list.
Other important factors such as payout schedule, minimum amounts, consistency, scaling, and taxation all have their own sections and dated sources. A closing checklist lets traders test any funded stock account evaluation before paying for it.

What Is A Profit Split?
A profit split is the share of trading profit a funded trader keeps, while the firm keeps the rest. Beginners ask what a profit split in prop trading is, and why a firm would share at all. As a result, both sides want the same thing. The firm collects its share only when traders earn.
🔗 Stock Prop Firms: Evaluation, Funding, Profit, and Payout Rules Explained
Reasons Behind Profit Sharing In Prop Trading Companies
This profit-sharing concept is possible due to the fact that the company provides the buying power, and the trader makes the decisions. So what happens after the trader passes the test? For example, the firm tracks eligible profit across a payout period. Then it pays the agreed percentage.

Virtual Capital And Real Payouts
Many beginners picture a funded stock account as real money sitting at a broker in their name. Is a funded account payout the same as real market profit? Not quite. Funded accounts use virtual capital; therefore, a payout works as a performance reward, not a market gain.

How A Profit Split Works: Calculation And Examples
The Profit Split Formula
The arithmetic is almost embarrassingly simple. Any trader can check a payout with a pocket calculator. No spreadsheet required. So what does a 70/30 profit split mean on payout day? The trader keeps 70% of eligible profit; in contrast, the firm keeps the remaining 30%.

Calculating A Trader’s Share
Take Trade The Pool’s published 70/30 profit split and run one payout through it. How does a trader calculate a share of profits? Multiply. For example, $3,000 of eligible profit times 70% gives the trader $2,100 and leaves the firm $900. Simple, but worth seeing once. The table below shows each step.

Evaluation Fees Sit Outside The Split
The evaluation fee trips up many beginners because traders pay it long before any profit exists. Trade The Pool’s program page listed $230 to $285 for a $50,000 evaluation on October 7, 2026. However, that is a separate upfront cost. The 70/30 split applies only to eligible profit. Keep the two apart.

Why a Higher Split Is Not Always Better
Here the headline starts to wobble. A September 2026 PropFirmMap study found 26 of 169 firms advertising 90% or more charged extra for it. In 16 of those 26, the default sat at 80%. Is a 90% profit split always better than 70%? However, no, because timing, minimums, and rules decide what arrives.

The Truth About 100% Profit Splits
Then comes the 100% profit split, the most misread number in the category. Are 100% profit splits real? Most are, but limited. Profit Split Payouts and US Taxes
How US Prop Firm Payouts Are Usually Taxed
This part only gives general education; it does not constitute personal tax advice. All assertions made here have dates and sources attached to them. Is the income from prop firms taxable in the United States? Yes. Kathy Lien’s January 2026 Investing.com analysis describes payouts as business income, not capital gains.

Payouts Versus Capital Gains
Many stock traders already know capital gains from selling shares in a brokerage account. Prop firm payouts follow different rules. Are they taxed like stock market gains? In the US, payouts usually count as self-employment income; therefore, a 15.3% self-employment tax can apply too. That difference surprises many new funded traders.

Profit Split: Key Takeaways for Funded Traders
Profit split is a method for distributing the profit from trades between a funded trader and the organization that opens the trading account. Does profit split provide steady income? No.
A profit split applies only to profits earned within the rules; therefore, it never guarantees any income. Losing periods produce no payout at all.
The main formula to calculate the money involved is: Eligible profit times trader’s percentage = Gross payout. Nonetheless, the factors that will determine the amount to be received and the payout timing include minimums, consistency rules, scaling, and taxes.
